Keys, wallets and transactions

On the chain itself nobody has an account with a password. You hold a private key, and anything you sign with it, the whole network can check.

In one lineYour private key is your identity: it signs, everyone can check, and nobody can reset it.

01Keys

A private key is a very large random number that only you know. From it, software computes a public key that anyone may see, and an address (a shortened hash of the public key) that people send to. Going forward is easy; going back, from address to private key, is not practical with today's computers.

A wallet is just software that keeps your private keys and uses them. It does not hold coins: the chain records which address owns what, and the key is what lets you move it.

Most wallets show you a recovery phrase of 12 or 24 words when you set them up. Those words can recreate every key in the wallet, so the phrase is the key: whoever has it has the funds. If you keep crypto on an exchange instead, the exchange holds the keys for you, and you are trusting it like a bank.

02Signing a transaction

To send something, your wallet writes a transaction (“from this address, to that one, this amount”) and signs it with your private key. Every node checks the signature against your public key before accepting it. A changed transaction, or one signed with any other key, fails the check.

Your browser signs with ECDSA on the P-256 curve. Bitcoin and Ethereum sign on a different curve, secp256k1, which browsers do not offer (Bitcoin has also used Schnorr signatures on it since 2021).

03No reset button

Because the key is the only proof that you own something, losing it means losing access, and anyone who copies it can spend as you. There is no support desk that can reverse a signed, confirmed transaction. That is the price of not needing a bank, and the main reason people lose money: see Risks.
