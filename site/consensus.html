Consensus: agreeing without trust

  1. Wallets & appsLayer 4 of 4
  2. Layer 2Layer 3 of 4
  3. Smart contractsLayer 2 of 4
  4. ConsensusLayer 1 of 4

Thousands of computers that do not trust each other must agree on one list of transactions. There are three main ways to do it.

Proof of work

Bitcoin's answer: to add a block, a computer must find a number (a nonce) that makes the block's hash start with enough zeros. The only way is to guess, billions of times. Whoever finds one first adds the block and is paid for it. Rewriting history would mean redoing that work faster than everyone else combined.

The hash is SHA-256 of the data, a bar, and the nonce: data|nonce.

Bitcoin adjusts its difficulty so that, across the whole network, a block is found about every ten minutes.

Proof of stake

Instead of spending electricity, validators lock up the chain's own tokens as stake: a deposit they lose if they cheat. The protocol picks who proposes each block, and other validators vote on it. Ethereum switched from proof of work to proof of stake in September 2022.

BFT voting, and how many can fail

Some proof-of-stake chains, like those built on CometBFT, vote in rounds. A block is final, settled for good, once validators holding more than two thirds of the voting power sign it. So there is never a second, competing version of the chain (a fork) that you have to wait to see settled.

It stays safe as long as validators with fewer than one third of the voting power are faulty: crashed, lying or working together. That is the limit for any protocol of this kind, called Byzantine fault tolerance. On a real chain, voting power follows stake, so the third is a third of the stake, not of the machines.
